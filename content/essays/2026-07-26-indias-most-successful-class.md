Elites often overlook the institutional foundations of their success, instead attributing it to factors such as religion, race, culture, or ancient civilization. India’s English-speaking upper caste appears to be repeating this oversight.

For generations, this group has dominated many of India’s most influential professions, including academia, the civil service, medicine, engineering, and finance, and later expanded into the technology sector. Their descendants have become doctors in Britain, software engineers in Silicon Valley, researchers in American universities, and executives in multinational corporations.

Currently, many members of this social and political group support efforts to redefine India's educational and intellectual foundations. The institutions that facilitated their advancement are now often regarded as remnants of a colonial legacy to be transcended. Determining whether this movement constitutes national renewal or historical self-sabotage remains a central question for contemporary India.

## The Machine Britain Left Behind

Although the railway is often considered Britain’s most significant legacy in India, the more consequential impact was institutional. The British established universities, courts, a centralized bureaucracy, and a civil service that relied extensively on English for administration and higher education.

These institutions were products of colonial rule, reflecting and reinforcing the inequalities of that era. Access to English education and elite professions was far from equal. Educated upper-caste communities became disproportionately represented in many of these institutions, securing advantages that persisted into the decades following independence.

After independence in 1947, India rejected colonial rule but retained much of the existing administrative structure. While national symbols changed, the underlying institutional framework persisted. English continued to dominate higher education, scientific research, the judiciary, medicine, engineering, and the civil service. As a result, individuals with pre-existing educational capital entered independent India with a considerable advantage.

## The Human-Capital Supply Chain

India inherited an educational system that connected domestic talent to the global economy. This pathway began with English-medium schools, continued through universities, and rewarded achievement in highly competitive examinations. The system produced engineers, physicians, scientists, and administrators, ultimately supplying skilled professionals to the English-speaking world.

India’s primary export was not limited to software, but extended to human capital. Globalization significantly increased the value of this educational system. Indian engineers were employed in Silicon Valley, Indian doctors joined the NHS, Indian researchers contributed to American universities, and Indian technologists were recruited by global banks.

English emerged as the global language of opportunity, and India developed one of the world’s largest pools of English-speaking technical professionals. The educated upper and middle classes, who had invested in English education, benefited most consistently from this transformation.

## When the Politics Changed

Economic growth often fosters cultural confidence. As India’s economy strengthened, questions arose regarding the continued reliance on institutions inherited from colonial rule, particularly given India’s longstanding and rich civilizational history.

This issue warrants careful consideration. Nations possess the right to reassess educational priorities, and civilizations may seek to preserve their intellectual traditions. However, challenges emerge when cultural confidence leads to the disruption of established institutions.

In recent years, political discourse in India has increasingly emphasized indigenous knowledge systems, traditional medicine, and Indian languages, with the intention of diminishing the cultural influence of colonial institutions. Proponents regard this as a process of intellectual decolonization, while critics express concern that ideological motivations may alter institutions that have historically produced globally competitive scientists, engineers, physicians, and researchers. The long-term consequences of these changes remain uncertain.

## The Contradiction

This paradox is particularly evident in many ambitious Indian households. While parents express support for cultural revival, they also compete intensely for admission to English-medium schools. Although they emphasize civilizational pride, they encourage their children to master English, study computer science, prepare for international graduate programs, and pursue careers in global cities such as London, New York, Toronto, or Silicon Valley.

Political rhetoric in these communities often emphasizes intellectual independence from the West. Nevertheless, family aspirations continue to rely on Western universities, Western scientific standards, global labor markets, and English as the primary language of opportunity. While this tension is not unique to India, it is becoming increasingly pronounced.

## Institutions Matter More Than Nostalgia

Modern economies depend on robust institutions, including research universities, scientific inquiry, professional standards, engineering and medical education, academic freedom, and international collaboration. These frameworks require ongoing investment and are significantly easier to dismantle than to reconstruct.

Civilizations should preserve their philosophical traditions, languages, literature, and cultural heritage. However, maintaining cultural identity does not guarantee continued competitiveness. The most successful societies achieved prosperity not solely by honoring their past, but by establishing institutions that foster innovation in future generations.

## The Elite's Paradox

Throughout history, ruling classes have often misunderstood the foundations of their success. Many inherited institutions developed over generations, assumed these structures were permanent, and shifted focus to cultural symbols while neglecting the institutional mechanisms that sustained their prosperity.

India's educated elite now confronts a comparable challenge. If the institutions responsible for producing globally competitive professionals are weakened before effective alternatives are established, the primary burden will fall on the families who have historically benefited most from these institutions, rather than on political adversaries.

Some of history’s most significant ironies are self-inflicted. The central issue is not whether India should reclaim its civilizational identity, but whether it can simultaneously preserve the human-capital system that enabled millions of Indians to become global citizens. Should this system falter, those who have most relied on its continued success may be the first to experience its decline.
