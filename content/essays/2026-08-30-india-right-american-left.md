The seatbelt sign pinged, and the cabin of Flight AI-101 began its gentle, 35,000-foot descent toward JFK Airport.

In seat 14B, Shri Divyendra adjusted his neck pillow. If you were to ask Divyendra about his origins, he wouldn't start with a hospital in Delhi. He would tell you, with absolute earnestness, that his ancestors emerged directly from the head of the Creator Himself. Other people, he'd imply with a gentle sigh, were assembled from various secondary, less prestigious locations. He considered this a foundational historical fact and was routinely disappointed that modern society didn't discuss it more often.

Up until the aircraft crossed the Atlantic, Divyendra had spent his life as a passionate defender of hierarchy and tradition. "Society must respect its roots," he had declared to his cousins at his farewell dinner. "Ancient wisdom must be preserved. Western ideas are destroying civilization."

But somewhere over the ocean, a miracle occurred. A silent, internal software upgrade took place.

By the time the wheels touched the tarmac in New York, Divyendra had morphed into a tireless champion of diversity, inclusion, and social justice. Within a week at his new corporate job in New Jersey, he was leading a workshop, carefully explaining how inherited privilege creates unfair social outcomes. None of his American colleagues realized that less than seventy-two hours prior, Divyendra had been defending inherited privilege as the bedrock of societal stability.

Political scientists would call this a blatant contradiction. Divyendra simply called it context.

In America, he was a minority; in India, he was the majority. Coincidentally, his political philosophy always favored whichever group currently contained Divyendra. Experts might study this for years, but he had a much simpler explanation: "It is called consistency."

For years, life in New Jersey was a symphony of harmony. Every Friday, Divyendra joined his colleagues for lunch. Around the table sat a vibrant mosaic of humanity: Christians, Muslims, atheists, vegans, and unrepentant beef eaters. Divyendra chatted happily with all of them, seamlessly moving from project deadlines to mortgage rates and vacation plans. The dietary habits of his companions didn't cause him a microsecond of emotional discomfort. He even clinked glasses with them at happy hour.

Then came the winter holidays, and Divyendra boarded a flight back to India.

The moment the plane touched down at Delhi Airport, the software rebooted. Suddenly, the dietary choices of absolute strangers became a matter of existential national importance.

"Cow is our mother!" he proclaimed passionately over tea at a family gathering.

The very same man who had spent years peacefully sharing conference rooms, office parties, and lunch tables with beef-eating colleagues now spoke as though civilization itself was hanging by a thread over a dinner plate.

A puzzled cousin, who followed Divyendra on social media, cornered him in the hallway. "Divyendra, I don't understand. You didn't seem to have any objection to this in New Jersey. You literally post photos at steakhouses with your boss."

Divyendra smiled benignly, adjusting his traditional kurta. "The situations are completely different."

"What exactly is the difference?" the cousin pressed.

Divyendra thought carefully, channeling the ancient wisdom of his ancestors. "The difference is that in one place I am a minority, and in the other place I am part of the majority."

The cousin waited for the rest of the explanation. The silence stretched. No further explanation arrived.

Perhaps, indeed, no further explanation was needed. For Divyendra possessed a rare, modern superpower: his ideology was entirely GPS-enabled. The moment his airplane landed, his principles automatically updated to match the local political climate.

At JFK Airport: "Question authority!"
At Delhi Airport: "Questioning authority is anti-national!"

In America: "Minority voices must be protected at all costs!"
In India: "The majority culture is under threat and must be defended!"

The software was remarkably sophisticated. It always knew exactly which side of a debate placed Divyendra closest to power, status, or moral superiority.

And somehow, no matter where in the world his feet were planted, Divyendra remained absolutely, beautifully convinced that he was defending universal, unchanging principles. It was, by all accounts, a truly miraculous achievement.
